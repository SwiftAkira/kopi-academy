## Three parts of a logic

The course sums it up as: logic = syntax + semantics + calculus.

| Part | The question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Syntax** | Is this string a legal formula? | `P → ¬Q` is legal, `P → ∧ Q` is not |
| **Semantics** | What does the formula mean? That is its truth value under an interpretation. | With P true and Q true, `P → ¬Q` is false |
| **Calculus** | Which procedure works the truth out? | A truth table now, DPLL later |

## The five connectives

| P | Q | ¬P | P ∧ Q | P ∨ Q | P → Q | P ↔ Q |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | F | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | T | F | F |
| F | T | T | F | T | T | F |
| F | F | T | F | F | T | T |

Two columns cause most mistakes:

- `P → Q` is false in **one** row only: true left side, false right side. A false left side makes it true.
- `P ↔ Q` is true when both sides are **equal**, also when both are false.

Other spellings you may meet: `-` or `~` for ¬, `&` for ∧, `v` for ∨, `->` or ⇒ for →, and ⇔ for ↔. Lecture tables use 1 and 0. The exam wants capital T and F.

## Building a truth table

Let P be "it rains", Q "you carry an umbrella" and R "you get wet". Take the sentence "rain makes you wet, and an umbrella keeps you dry": `(P → R) ∧ (Q → ¬R)`.

1. Write the variables first. With n variables there are 2 to the power n rows, so 8 here.
2. Start with the all-T row and end with the all-F row. The rightmost variable changes fastest.
3. Add one column per sub-formula. Every negation gets its own column.
4. Fill in column by column. The whole formula comes last.

| P | Q | R | ¬R | P → R | Q → ¬R | (P → R) ∧ (Q → ¬R) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | F | T | F | F |
| T | T | F | T | F | T | F |
| T | F | T | F | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | F | T | F |
| F | T | T | F | T | F | F |
| F | T | F | T | T | T | T |
| F | F | T | F | T | T | T |
| F | F | F | T | T | T | T |

## Interpretations and models

An **interpretation** (the course also says validation function) `v` gives every variable a value. One row is one interpretation. Row 3 is `v(P) = True, v(Q) = False, v(R) = True`.

A **model** of a formula is an interpretation that makes it true. The formula above has 4 models: rows 3, 6, 7 and 8.

| Word | Meaning | Last column |
|---|---|---|
| **satisfiable** | at least one model | some T |
| **valid** (a tautology) | true under every interpretation | only T |
| **unsatisfiable** | no model at all | only F |

Our formula is satisfiable but not valid. Every valid formula is also satisfiable.

## Entailment

A **knowledge base** (KB) is a set of sentences that are taken as true, written like `{ P → R, Q → ¬R }`. A model of a KB makes **all** its sentences true at once, so the set behaves like one big conjunction. This KB has the same 4 models as the formula above.

`KB ⊨ α` is read "KB entails α". It means: α is true in **every** model of KB. To check it, look only at the rows where KB is true.

- Is `¬(P ∧ Q)` entailed? In rows 3, 6, 7 and 8, P and Q are never both true. Yes.
- Is `R` entailed? Row 6 is a model of KB, and R is false there. One counter-row is enough: no.

One link to remember for DPLL: `KB ⊨ α` holds exactly when KB together with `¬α` is unsatisfiable.
